<div class="prose">
  <p>Suppose someone grants everything above but still wants to hold their ground. They say: reality, at its deepest level, simply is. There is no further explanation. Some things just exist, and that is acceptable. This is not a contradiction. It is a coherent position — and it is the position the weak form of the principle implicitly rests on.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">That seems honest. Isn't it more intellectually humble to admit there might be no ultimate explanation than to insist there must be one?</p>
  <p>Ask a different question: what is the status of the claim "some things have no explanation"? Is that claim itself something that has a reason — a basis in evidence, logic, or argument? Or does it have no reason either? There are only two options. If the claim has a reason, then it participates in the very framework of intelligibility it is trying to deny. Reason is already at work in the denial. The principle being refused is already operative in the act of refusing it. If the claim has no reason — if it is simply asserted, without argument, as a brute declaration — then it carries no rational weight. It is indistinguishable from noise. A position that cannot be given reasons for is not a philosophical stance. It is a sound.</p>
  <p>This is not saying the claim is self-contradictory. It is not. It is saying something more precise: the claim cannot be made as a rational argument without already presupposing the framework it is trying to reject. Any argument, on any topic, presupposes that concepts have stable meaning, that statements can be true or false, and that reasons support conclusions. These are not scientific assumptions — they are the preconditions of thought itself. To argue that not everything is intelligible is already to assume that argument is intelligible, that the claim being made has meaning, and that the reasons given for it count. The denial of intelligibility either relies on intelligibility or it has no force.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">Isn't this just wordplay? The brute fact position doesn't need to argue for itself — it just accepts that some things are unexplained.</p>
  <p>If it does not argue for itself, it cannot ask to be taken seriously as a philosophical position. A claim that neither needs reasons nor offers them is not humble — it is inert. The moment it enters a conversation, it borrows the currency of rational discourse: it asserts something, it implies that the assertion is true rather than false, it expects the listener to weigh it. All of that is intelligibility. There is no way to say "not everything needs an explanation" from outside the practice of explanation. The denial of the strong form of the principle is not coherently available as a reasoned conclusion. It is only available as a decision to stop reasoning — and decisions to stop reasoning do not refute arguments. They evade them.</p>
