<div class="prose">
  <p>The argument has established that a necessary, non-contingent cause of the universe exists. The next question is what kind of thing it could be. The most common instinct is to reach for something impersonal: a field, an energy, a primordial force, a mathematical principle. These feel less committal than a personal being, and they carry the quiet authority of physics.</p>
  <p>But a force is not a cause in the required sense. A force is a measurable quantity — it has magnitude, direction, and units. It is, by definition, something that acts on other things. Which means it presupposes those other things. A gravitational field without mass to act on is not a field at all; it is nothing. A force is always a relation between things that already exist. It cannot be what explains why there are things to relate to in the first place.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">What about energy? Energy is more fundamental than force — couldn't a primordial energy be the necessary ground?</p>
  <p>Energy is a quantity. It is measured, finite in any bounded system, conserved across transformations. But everything measurable and finite is contingent: it could have been a different quantity, a different configuration, distributed differently. A specific quantity of energy is not something that exists by logical necessity — it could have been otherwise. And if it could have been otherwise, it requires an explanation for why it is as it is. That explanation cannot be energy itself. We are back to the original problem, now one level up.</p>
  <p>The deeper point is that forces, fields, and energies are all attributes. They are predicated of something — they do not stand on their own. The phrase "there is a field" already presupposes something in which the field exists, something of which the field is a property. Attributes cannot ground themselves. A quality requires a subject. Whatever the necessary cause is, it cannot be merely an attribute: it must be a being — something that exists in its own right, not as a property of something else.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">What about mathematical objects — could the necessary ground be a necessary abstract object, like a logical truth or a number?</p>
  <p>Mathematical truths are necessarily true: 2 + 2 = 4 could not have been otherwise. But necessary truth is not the same as causal power. A theorem does not produce anything. "The universe exists because prime numbers are infinite" is not an explanation — it is a category error. Abstract objects describe structure; they do not create it. Whatever caused the universe to exist must have had the power to bring something into being — and abstract objects, by their nature, have no such power. They are eternal and necessary, but inert.</p>
</div>
