  <p>The necessary being exists by its own nature — it cannot fail to exist, and its existence depends on nothing outside itself. This is what it means to be necessary. Now consider what follows from this. A being that exists necessarily and is the ground of all contingent things cannot itself be deficient in any way. Deficiency means lacking something that could be present. But a being that exists necessarily and independently has no "something missing" — there is nothing outside it that it depends on, and nothing it is missing from its own nature. In the classical tradition this is expressed by saying the necessary being is perfect — not perfect in the colloquial sense of "very good," but in the technical sense of complete, lacking nothing.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">Perfection is a human concept. We project it onto God — but why should the necessary being be perfect rather than merely sufficient?</p>
  <p>Perfection here is not a human evaluation. It is a structural consequence of necessary existence. A being that is necessary and self-subsistent cannot be deficient, because deficiency requires dependence — lacking something means needing something from outside. But the necessary being is precisely the one that needs nothing from outside. The absence of deficiency is not a compliment we pay to it. It follows from what it is. Now, goodness — understood as the possession of being and all its perfections — is something that can be present or absent. A being that possesses all perfections, because it cannot lack them, is therefore good. Not morally good in the first instance, but ontologically good: fully actualized, complete in its being, lacking nothing.</p>
  <p>The moral dimension follows from this. If the necessary being has intelligence and wisdom, as established in the previous two steps, then it has will — an orientation toward what is good. But a being that is itself the fullness of being cannot be oriented toward deficiency. Its will cannot aim at corruption, limitation, or nothingness, because that would mean it is in some sense trying to diminish what it is. The necessary being's will is necessarily aligned with being and its perfections. That alignment is what goodness means in the moral sense.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">If God is good, why is there evil? If he could have prevented it and didn't, he is either not powerful enough or not good enough.</p>
  <p>Evil is not a substance or a positive thing that must be created. It is an absence — the absence of a good that ought to be present. Blindness is not a thing; it is the absence of sight in something capable of seeing. Cruelty is not a created object; it is the absence of the goodness that a rational being ought to have. A being that is fully good does not create evil, because evil is not a thing to be created. It is a deficiency that arises in contingent beings that can fall short of what they ought to be.</p>
  <p>Why allow that falling short? Because the highest form of goodness in a creature is not compelled behaviour but free orientation toward the good. A being that is compelled to act rightly has no moral worth — it is an automaton. Freedom is a perfection; and genuine freedom includes the possibility of its misuse. The existence of moral evil is not evidence against a good creator. It is evidence that the creator produced beings capable of genuine goodness — which requires the genuine possibility of its opposite.</p>
