<div class="prose">
  <p>The attributes established so far — necessary existence, causal power, intelligence, wisdom, goodness — converge on a further consequence that is less often stated but follows with equal rigour. A being that has intelligence and wisdom has, by those very facts, something analogous to will: an orientation toward possible states of affairs, a capacity to select among them, a relationship to what is and what could be. Will is not an additional faculty layered on top of intelligence. It is what intelligence looks like in a being that is not compelled by its nature to produce one fixed outcome.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">Will and intention are psychological concepts. Why apply them to something that transcends the physical universe?</p>
  <p>Because the argument requires them. We established that the universe is contingent — it could have been different. We established that the necessary being is the sufficient reason for this particular universe rather than another. A sufficient reason for a contingent outcome, in a being that is not compelled to produce only one outcome, is precisely what we mean by a choice. And a being that chooses — that can orient itself toward one possible state of affairs rather than another — has what philosophy has always called will. Not human will with its biochemical substrate. The capacity to be directed toward a specific outcome among possible ones, for reasons that are grounded in understanding.</p>
  <p>A being with intelligence, wisdom, and will — one that knows, deliberates, and acts — is what philosophy calls a person. Not a person in the sense of a human individual with a body and a history, but person in the sense of a subject of knowledge and agency: something that knows and does, rather than merely existing as an inert fact. Impersonal things do not know or choose. They have states, not acts. The necessary being has acts — it brought this contingent universe into being rather than another — and that is the mark of personhood.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">Assuming a personal being — why would it communicate? Why should we expect a revelation?</p>
  <p>The argument does not assume a revelation. It shows why revelation is not incoherent given what has been established. If the necessary being is personal — if it knows, chooses, and acts — then communication is within its nature. Persons communicate. The question is not whether communication is possible for such a being, but whether it has communicated, and if so, how we would identify it. A being that is good and that created rational creatures would have reason to communicate: goodness shares itself. A being that is wise would communicate in ways that are coherent and recognizable. A being that is the ground of all meaning would not communicate in noise.</p>
  <p>This is where the argument from reason terminates and a different kind of inquiry begins. The question of whether this personal being has communicated — and which of the historical claims to be that communication are coherent and credible — cannot be settled by the argument that brought us here. It requires examining those claims on their own terms: their internal coherence, their historical grounding, their relationship to what the necessary being's nature would lead us to expect. That examination is Chapter IV.</p>
</div>
