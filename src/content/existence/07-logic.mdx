<div class="prose">
  <p>At this point the objection is raised: the principle of causality is not a scientifically proven law — it is a metaphysical principle, and therefore carries no force in serious inquiry. We should stick to what science can confirm.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">That seems reasonable. Science is our most reliable method for understanding reality. Why bring in philosophy when we have it?</p>
  <p>Because science is built on logic — not the other way around. Physics operates within the universe, describing how its structures evolve and its parts interact. When the question is "why does the universe exist at all?", that question is structurally prior to any physical description. The tools for answering it are not experimental. To refuse a logical argument because it hasn't been scientifically verified is to apply science in a domain it was never designed to reach — and to forget what science itself is standing on.</p>
  <p>The laws below are not scientific hypotheses that science has tested. They are preconditions of rational thought — the foundations on which science is built, and without which no scientific inference would hold. Science uses them; it cannot prove them without circularity; and it cannot survive their denial.</p>
</div>

<div class="interactive" style="max-width:600px;margin-top:28px;">
  <div class="law-card">
    <div class="law-name">Law of Non-Contradiction</div>
    <div class="law-statement">"A proposition cannot be both true and false at the same time and in the same respect."</div>
    <div class="law-body">Every scientific inference depends on this. You cannot deny it without using it — denial asserts something as true, which presupposes the law. It is not a result of science. It is the ground on which science stands.</div>
  </div>
  <div class="law-card">
    <div class="law-name">Law of Identity</div>
    <div class="law-statement">"A thing is identical to itself (A = A)."</div>
    <div class="law-body">Science assumes objects have stable identities across observations. Denying it makes empirical observation incoherent. Cannot be proven scientifically without circularity.</div>
  </div>
  <div class="law-card">
    <div class="law-name">Law of Excluded Middle</div>
    <div class="law-statement">"For any proposition P, either P is true or its negation is true."</div>
    <div class="law-body">Scientific hypotheses must be confirmed or disconfirmed. Without this law, no test has a definite result. Denying it is self-contradictory — the denial is itself a P-or-not-P statement.</div>
  </div>
  <div class="law-card">
    <div class="law-name">Principle of Sufficient Reason</div>
    <div class="law-statement">"Every fact or truth has an explanation or reason why it is so."</div>
    <div class="law-body">Science assumes events have causes and patterns have explanations. Without this, any result could be true for no reason, making argument impossible. Denying it is self-defeating — your denial would itself be a claim true for no reason.</div>
  </div>
  <div style="padding:13px 16px;border:1px solid var(--accent2);border-radius:5px;background:var(--accent-glow);margin-top:4px;">
    <div style="font-family:'Crimson Pro',serif;font-style:italic;font-size:0.96rem;color:var(--accent);line-height:1.65;">To deny these principles because they are not scientifically proven is to deny the framework that makes science possible. Denying logic in the name of science is science betraying itself.</div>
  </div>
