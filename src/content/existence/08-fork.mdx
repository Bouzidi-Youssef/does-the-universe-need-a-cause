<div class="prose">
  <p>The inquiry arrives at a genuine fork. Neither path is ruled out by science — this is expected, because science cannot speak to what lies outside its own domain. But the two paths are not equally positioned when evaluated by logic alone.</p>
  <p>One path follows the principle of sufficient reason to its conclusion. Every contingent thing — every thing that might not have existed, or might have been different — requires an explanation in something prior. The chain cannot regress infinitely through contingent causes; an infinite regress of contingent explanations does not produce an explanation, it merely defers one forever. Therefore something exists that is not contingent: something whose existence is not dependent on anything prior, something that cannot fail to exist. This is what philosophers call the necessary being. The argument doesn't specify its nature — only that it must exist, and that it must be sufficient to explain all contingent existence.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">But why can't the laws of physics themselves just be a brute fact — existing without explanation? Why must there be something beyond them?</p>
  <p>They can be — and that is the second path. It is logically coherent in the sense that it produces no immediate contradiction. But it pays a price: the most fundamental question — why is there something rather than nothing? — is declared unanswerable. Not because we haven't found the answer, but because there is nothing to find. Rationality is not reaching a terminus here. It is being withdrawn. The principle of sufficient reason, which underlies all causal reasoning and all science, is suspended precisely where it matters most.</p>
  <p>Both are philosophical commitments. The difference is what each does with the demand for explanation: one honours it to its terminus; the other withdraws it.</p>
