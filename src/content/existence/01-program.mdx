<div class="prose">
  <p>Imagine you write a C++ program. Every time you compile and run it, it simulates a full universe from scratch. The Big Bang happens, physics takes over, everything unfolds. Stars ignite, planets cool, billions of years pass in an instant of compute time.</p>
  <p>Now press Run. Would you get life on the first attempt? Or would you need to press it thousands of times? Millions? The question sounds simple, but it cracks open something very deep about existence.</p>
  <p>In this first version of the program, the constants are already hardcoded: the speed of light, gravity, electromagnetism — all set to the values we've measured. Each run plays by identical rules but with different random initial conditions. The only variable is the seeding of the primordial plasma.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">But how often does life actually appear? Surely a universe this vast makes it common?</p>
  <p>Even with constants locked in, scientists genuinely disagree. Some think life is nearly inevitable — hundreds of billions of galaxies, each with hundreds of billions of stars, and organic molecules form spontaneously in space. Others point to the Fermi Paradox: if life is so easy, why is the observable universe so silent? The three positions to the right are all scientifically defensible. None has been ruled out. They tell us how many runs we'd need — not whether a cause is required.</p>
  <p>Notice what this first framing already reveals: even when the universe is pre-loaded with life-friendly rules, the emergence of life is not guaranteed. And the deeper question — <em>why those rules are set the way they are</em> — hasn't even been asked yet.</p>
