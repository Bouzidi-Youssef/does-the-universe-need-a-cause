<div class="prose">
  <p>Science is constrained to the observable. Logic is not. When the question is why there is something rather than nothing, physics takes us to the edge and then hits a wall. What lies beyond the wall is not a scientific question. It is a question for logic — which is not built on science, but which science is built on.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">But science being silent here doesn't mean logic has an answer. Shouldn't we simply say "we don't know" and leave it there?</p>
  <p>Saying "we don't know" is honest. Saying "therefore there is no cause" or "therefore the question has no answer" is not — it is a conclusion smuggled in under the cover of humility. Ignorance of a cause is not the same as the absence of one. The two are not synonyms, and treating them as equivalent is not intellectual caution. It is abandoning the question while pretending to have answered it.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">Perhaps the principle of sufficient reason simply doesn't have to apply everywhere — maybe some things just are.</p>
  <p>Then you must choose where to suspend it — and you need a reason for that choice. If you suspend it at the level of physical laws, why not at the level of individual events? If some things can simply exist without explanation, what prevents any experimental result from being one of those things? The principle of sufficient reason is not an optional feature of rational inquiry. It is the condition under which rational inquiry is possible at all. Suspending it is not a scientific position. It is the dissolution of the framework within which science operates.</p>
  <p>What reason compels, then, is this: the universe exists; its existence is contingent; contingent existence demands explanation; the chain of contingent causes cannot regress forever; therefore something necessary exists. The character of that necessary being is a further question — one that logic alone cannot resolve, and where other paths of inquiry begin. But the logical demand for a first cause is not a leap of faith. It is the terminus of a chain of reasoning that begins with the most modest observation possible — that there is something rather than nothing — and follows the principle of sufficient reason wherever it leads.</p>
</div>
