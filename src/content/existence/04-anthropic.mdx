<div class="prose">
  <p>At this point the obvious response presents itself. We observe a life-permitting universe not because such universes are common, but because only such universes can be observed. This is the Anthropic Principle: any observer will necessarily find themselves in conditions compatible with their existence.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">Doesn't that dissolve the problem? Of course we see a life-permitting universe — we wouldn't be here to ask otherwise.</p>
  <p>It is a genuine logical truth — but far more limited than it appears. The Anthropic Principle is a selection effect, not a probability claim. It tells us we shouldn't be surprised to observe life-compatible conditions, given that we exist to observe anything. What it does not tell us is how likely those conditions were, whether the program was run once or many times, or what explains those conditions in the first place. It shifts the framing of the observation. It does not explain the observation.</p>
  <p>Consider: if the program ran only once, and the constants happened to land in the narrow life-permitting range, that single successful outcome is still genuinely improbable. The Anthropic Principle doesn't dissolve that improbability — it only says that, improbable or not, we can only ever observe the successes.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">But what if the program ran many times — a multiverse? Then rare events are expected, and the reasoning fully applies.</p>
  <p>Exactly — and that is the unresolved variable. The Anthropic Principle does real work only if there is a large ensemble of universes. But the multiverse is not an established fact. It is a theoretical framework suggested by certain models, none of which are confirmed. The honest answer to "how many times was the program run?" is: we do not know.</p>
</div>
