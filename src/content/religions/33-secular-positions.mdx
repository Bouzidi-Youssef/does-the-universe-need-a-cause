  <p>The religious traditions examined so far have been tested against criteria derived from what reason established about the necessary being. But there are positions that do not arrive at a personal God at all — and which therefore never reach the point where those criteria apply. They are not filtered by the five conditions of Chapter IV. They are filtered by the earlier argument: the conclusion, in Chapters I and II, that a necessary, non-contingent cause of the universe must exist.</p>
  <p><b style="color:#e0dbd2;">Atheism</b> denies that any god exists. This is the position that Chapters I and II were specifically constructed to address. The argument from contingency does not prove any particular religion correct; it does establish that a necessary, uncaused explanation for the universe's existence must exist. The atheist position is not that the argument is weak — it is that the conclusion is false. To hold that position, a specific response to each step in the argument is needed: either the universe is not contingent, or contingent things do not require explanation, or an infinite regress does explain the whole, or brute facts are acceptable at the foundation. Each of those responses was examined in Chapters I and II. Declining to respond to them is not the same as demonstrating they fail.</p>
  <p><b style="color:#e0dbd2;">Agnosticism</b> suspends judgment — not enough evidence either way to affirm or deny. That is an epistemically honest stance to take before an argument has been examined. Once the argument has been made, declining to engage with its specific steps is no longer suspension of judgment — it is deferral. "We cannot know" is not a counterargument to "here is a case that a necessary cause exists, and here are the responses to the main objections." If the argument has a flaw, the flaw can be located and named. Agnosticism after an argument is a choice to leave the evidence unweighed.</p>
  <p><b style="color:#e0dbd2;">Deism</b> accepts a creator God but denies that he communicates, intervenes, or concerns himself with his creation after the initial act. This is the position that Chapter III was specifically built to address. A being that is good — in the sense established there, as possessing all perfections and oriented toward the good of what it creates — does not produce rational creatures and then remain permanently silent about the purpose and terms of their existence. Deism accepts one half of the argument and declines the other. That declination requires its own case, which deism does not typically provide: an argument showing that divine goodness is consistent with permanent silence toward the created beings the good being chose to bring into existence.</p>
  <p><b style="color:#e0dbd2;">Pantheism</b> — the position that God and the universe are identical — was examined in Chapter III. The necessary being cannot be the universe, because the universe is contingent and the necessary being is not. Identifying them eliminates the necessary being rather than explaining the universe: what was left is a contingent thing relabeled as God, which does not satisfy the explanatory requirement that motivated positing a necessary being in the first place.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">Isn't it possible to hold one of these positions and still find the arguments in the earlier chapters unconvincing?</p>
  <p>Yes — and this is worth saying clearly. None of the filtering here works by decree. It works by showing that a position either directly contradicts what was argued in earlier chapters, or fails to engage with specific steps in those arguments in a way that would need to be answered for the position to survive. Someone who finds the original arguments unpersuasive has not arrived at the filtering stage; they have a prior disagreement with the argument that grounds it. That prior disagreement is what the earlier chapters exist to address. The filtering in this chapter only applies to someone who has followed those arguments to their conclusion.</p>
