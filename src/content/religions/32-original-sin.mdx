<div class="prose">
  <p>The doctrinal architecture of salvation in the mainstream Christian tradition rests on a specific account of what went wrong with humanity — and what was needed to repair it. Adam sinned. His sin was not simply a mistake that damaged his own standing; it introduced a condition of guilt and corruption that all subsequent human beings inherit by virtue of being human. The entire mechanism of salvation — the incarnation, the crucifixion, the atoning death — is constructed on the premise that humanity carries a debt it cannot pay and cannot escape, because the debt belongs to the species by nature, not by personal choice.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">This is central to Christian theology — what is the logical objection beyond disagreeing with the premise?</p>
  <p>Chapter III established two attributes of the necessary being that bear directly on this. The first is justice. A just being does not assign guilt without agency. Guilt is the condition of being responsible for a wrong — and responsibility requires that the one held responsible was capable of choosing otherwise, was the one who made the choice, and is being held to account for that particular choice. Adam's act was Adam's. I was not present for it, did not choose it, had no capacity to influence it, and did not exist when it occurred. Being held guilty of it — not influenced by it, not living in a world whose conditions it altered, but genuinely guilty, in need of an atoning payment — is structurally inconsistent with the kind of justice attributed to the necessary being.</p>
  <p>The second attribute is the test. Chapter III noted that a just God who creates rational beings creates them with genuine freedom — the kind of freedom whose exercise has real stakes. The test is individual: what does this person, with this capacity, choose in the situations available to them? Inheriting a condition of guilt from an ancestor collapses the test before it begins. The candidate arrives at the examination already in debt for a wrong they did not commit, needing rescue from a burden whose source preceded their existence. That is not a test of individual freedom. It is a sentence passed before the trial.</p>
  <p>The atonement mechanism carries a further structural difficulty. The Christian account holds that God's justice required a payment, and that Jesus — God-in-human-form — made that payment by dying. But if God is the creditor and God arranges the payment, God is both demanding and supplying what He demands, both requiring satisfaction and providing it. This is not logically incoherent — one person can pay another's debt — but it is internally puzzling in a specific way: if God could arrange to forgive the debt by providing the payment Himself, what was the need for a mechanism at all? Why not simply forgive? The answer given within the tradition — that God's justice cannot be satisfied without payment — raises the question of why a being with unlimited capacity has justice that can only work one way, without the option of a direct pardon to the penitent.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">The tradition distinguishes between guilt and damage — original sin is about a damaged nature, not a legal charge passed to each person.</p>
  <p>Some readings of the doctrine do exactly this — Augustine's reading differs from the Eastern Orthodox account, and modern Catholic theology has further refinements. What is worth noting is that the objection above rests entirely on what was already established: a just God does not hold individuals guilty for what they did not do. If the tradition's own best reading of original sin is that it concerns damaged nature rather than inherited guilt, then the objection about justice is mitigated. But then a different problem opens: the atoning mechanism was the solution to inherited guilt. If there is no inherited guilt — only a damaged nature — then what, precisely, required an atoning death rather than, for instance, a healing? The structure of the solution only makes sense if the problem is the one the objection targets.</p>
