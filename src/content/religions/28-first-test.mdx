<div class="prose">
  <p>The first criterion — pure monotheism — does more work than any other, because it tests a tradition's basic architecture rather than one clause within it. A tradition that does not present a single, transcendent, personal creator fails immediately, whatever else it offers: ethical depth, historical influence, the size of its following. None of that substitutes for the specific claim being tested here.</p>
  <p><b style="color:#e0dbd2;">Buddhism</b>, in its classical formulations, has no creator god. The Buddha is a teacher who found a path, not a deity; nirvana is a state to be reached, not a being to be known. Whatever spiritual value the tradition claims for itself, it does not attempt to answer the question this entire inquiry began with — why a contingent universe exists at all. A path to liberation with no liberator behind it does not meet the test, whatever it offers on its own terms.</p>
  <p><b style="color:#e0dbd2;">Taoism</b> and <b style="color:#e0dbd2;">Confucianism</b> fail for a related reason. The Tao is described as an impersonal principle underlying reality's patterns — not a knowing, willing agent. Confucianism is substantially an ethical and social philosophy, without a developed claim to revelation from a personal deity at all. Neither proposes the kind of being this test was built to examine, so neither reaches the point where the remaining four criteria would even apply.</p>
  <p><b style="color:#e0dbd2;">Hinduism's</b> traditions are diverse, but a widely held core affirms both a single ultimate reality (Brahman) and a vast pantheon of personal deities understood as its expressions. Held together, the two halves sit in tension against this criterion: either the deities are genuinely many, in which case unity fails outright, or they are only appearances of the one, in which case the personal gods so central to popular devotion are not, in the end, fully real — describing them as "aspects" relocates the tension rather than resolving it.</p>
  <p><b style="color:#e0dbd2;">Zoroastrianism's</b> classical dualism sets a good principle against an opposing, genuinely destructive one, locked in real cosmic conflict. Whatever this explains about evil's persistence, it does so by dividing ultimate power — the specific thing at stake in the case, built in Chapter III, for a single being with unlimited power.</p>
  <p><b style="color:#e0dbd2;">Jainism</b> has no creator god and centers on non-violence and individual liberation through ascetic discipline, leaving the origin question unaddressed by design. <b style="color:#e0dbd2;">Sikhism</b> affirms a single God, but describes that God as immanent within all things rather than categorically beyond them — the same transcendence tension examined in Chapter III. <b style="color:#e0dbd2;">Mandaeism</b> and <b style="color:#e0dbd2;">Yazidism</b> each affirm a high god in principle, but carry theological layers — a gnostic cosmology in one case, veneration of a central angelic figure in the other — that complicate a straightforward monotheism, and neither tradition's founding text survives in a form its own adherents present as fully public and mass-transmitted. The polytheisms of the ancient world fail at the simplest level of the test and, in any case, have no living communities left to examine.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">Isn't it unfair to lump traditions this different together in one section?</p>
  <p>They're grouped by outcome, not by content. Each fails the same first test for its own distinct internal reason, addressed above on its own terms. Grouping them doesn't erase those differences — it reflects that once a tradition fails the first criterion, the remaining four don't need to be examined for it individually.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">Buddhism and Taoism aren't really making a claim about God one way or the other — is it fair to test them as if they were?</p>
  <p>This inquiry's opening chapters concluded that a necessary, personal cause explains the universe's existence at all. Given that conclusion, every worldview is implicitly answering the question, whether or not it frames itself as theistic — silence on it is itself a kind of answer, and this method tests that answer like any other. A tradition can be a rich ethical or contemplative practice and still fail this specific test, because the test asks only whether it identifies the being the earlier chapters argued for — not whether it has value on other terms.</p>
