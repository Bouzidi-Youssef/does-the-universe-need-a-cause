<div class="prose">
  <p>Chapter IV closed with six conditions a genuine revelation must satisfy: internal consistency, external consistency, miracle, preservation, clarity, and completeness. Applied to actual traditions, three of these collapse naturally into a single doctrinal check, leaving five practical criteria to run each candidate through:</p>
  <p><b style="color:#e0dbd2;">Pure monotheism</b> — a single, transcendent, personal creator, with no partner, no incarnation, no indwelling. <b style="color:#e0dbd2;">Consistency with reason</b> — no contradiction, internal or against what was already established about God's nature. <b style="color:#e0dbd2;">Preservation</b> — an unbroken, attested chain from the text's origin to today. <b style="color:#e0dbd2;">Completeness</b> — coverage of what a final message would need to address, without later patchwork. <b style="color:#e0dbd2;">Miracle</b> — evidence beyond ordinary human capacity, tied to the one making the claim.</p>
  <p>None of these five is invented for the occasion. Each unpacks an attribute the earlier chapters already argued for. Monotheism follows from the necessary being's unity. Consistency follows from its wisdom. Preservation and completeness follow from the same wisdom applied to the act of sending a message at all — a wise sender does not deliver something and let it be corrupted, or leave out what the message was for. Miracle follows from the simple fact that a claim requires evidence, or it is indistinguishable from any other claim.</p>
  <p>The method itself is mechanical, almost deliberately so: take a tradition, apply the five criteria in turn, and if it fails any single one, set it aside — regardless of how well it performs on the rest. A tradition does not get partial credit for meeting four of five. The criteria are conjunctive, because a wise, one, honest God is either fully consistent with a message or the message is not fully His.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">Isn't this circular — don't you already know where you want to land, and grade everything else down to get there?</p>
  <p>The method starts from the criteria, and the criteria were derived in the previous chapter from what reason had already established about God's nature — not from any one tradition's self-description. Objectivity here means exactly one thing: the same five criteria apply to every tradition examined, without exception. If the tradition this inquiry eventually favors failed even one of them, it would be set aside on the same grounds as any other. That test is applied to it too, later in this chapter.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">Why five criteria, and not ten — or two?</p>
  <p>Each of the five is necessary to what a genuine revelation is for: a trustworthy message from a single wise God. Drop any one and something a genuine revelation would need goes unchecked. Add an unrelated sixth, and it would need its own derivation from reason — asserting a new criterion because one particular tradition happens to satisfy it is the very move this method is built to rule out.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">Why not test religions empirically, the way a scientist tests a hypothesis?</p>
  <p>Religions are not repeatable lab phenomena, and a private religious experience is not something a third party can check — which is exactly why it cannot serve as a public criterion. The five criteria are objective in a different, available sense: each is checked against public evidence — a text, a transmission record, a historical claim — that anyone can examine, rather than against something only available from the inside.</p>
</div>
