<div class="prose">
  <p>Running every serious candidate through the five criteria, the field narrows in stages. The non-theistic traditions — Buddhism, Taoism, Confucianism, Jainism — are set aside at the first criterion, since they do not present a single, transcendent, personal creator at all. The traditions that present multiple deities, or a divine principle indistinct from the natural world, are set aside at the same point on the same grounds. Zoroastrianism divides the supreme power between two ultimate principles and fails there. These are not close calls — the criterion is met or it is not, and the traditions above do not attempt to meet it as defined.</p>
  <p>Judaism affirms a single God, but the claim that God's covenant is exclusive to one ethnic lineage — that the message is addressed to a particular people rather than to humanity — conflicts with the justice attribute established in Chapter III. A just, wise being that creates all human beings and holds all of them to account does not release a message of guidance to a fraction of them while leaving the rest unaddressed. That framing may be contested within the tradition itself — whether the covenant was ever meant to exclude or only to begin with one community — but in the classical reading, the problem is real, and the historical text as preserved also fails the unbroken chain test, acknowledged within the tradition's own scholarship.</p>
  <p>Christianity affirms monotheism and universalism, but the doctrine of the Trinity presents three distinct persons sharing one divine essence in a way that, as argued in section 30, generates direct conflict with the law of identity applied to the attributes already established. The incarnation requires attributing simultaneously contradictory properties to a single subject in a way the hypostatic union names rather than resolves. The preservation criterion fails on documented evidence — admitted insertions, known variants resolved by scribal alteration, and the acknowledged loss of books the text itself cites. The original sin framework conflicts with divine justice as established. Multiple criteria fail; none of the responses available within the tradition dissolves the difficulties, though they honestly locate them.</p>
  <p>The Bahá'í Faith affirms monotheism and universalism but claims continuing revelation after Muhammad — a claim that requires independent justification, and which conflicts with the completeness criterion as it will be applied to Islam specifically: a complete message does not require supplementation. The Ahmadiyya tradition within Islam makes a comparable claim. Smaller traditions — the Druze, Alawites, and related communities — have theological positions that introduce divine indwelling or near-deification of human figures, failing the monotheism criterion directly, and most carry texts that are either secret in principle or not publicly mass-transmitted.</p>
  <p>What remains — the candidate that has not been set aside by any of the five criteria — is Islam in its classical, textually grounded form: pure monotheism with no exception, a text transmitted through a mass oral and written chain from the seventh century to the present with documented fidelity, a scope of guidance that addresses individual conduct, social organization, metaphysics, and the human relationship to the divine without a gap requiring later supplementation, a miracle claim tied specifically to the text's linguistic and structural properties rather than to events accessible only to contemporaries, and a conception of the divine that does not require attributing contradictory properties to a single subject.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">This sounds like a process engineered to produce one result — wasn't it built with Islam in mind from the start?</p>
  <p>The criteria were derived from what reason established about God's nature in Chapters II and III — prior to and independently of any tradition's self-description. A wise God sends a complete message; a just God sends it to all; an honest God does not produce a self-contradictory text; a God who communicates at all does not let that communication be corrupted. None of those derivations name Islam. They are logical consequences of the necessary being's attributes. If Islam satisfies all five, that is a finding from the application of the criteria, not a consequence of having built the criteria for that result. The test applies to Islam too — and finding that it passes is a result, not a premise.</p>
