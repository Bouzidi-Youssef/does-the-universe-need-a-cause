  <p>Judaism affirms one God with no partner, no incarnation, no indwelling. On that criterion it stands where every tradition in the previous section did not. But the first criterion alone is not sufficient. Two of the remaining four produce clear failures, and either one alone is enough to set it aside.</p>
  <p>The first is completeness. A final, universal message from God — one that addresses all of humanity — cannot subsequently require supplementation or replacement. If it did, it was not final. Judaism was followed, historically and in sequence, by Christianity and then by Islam, both of which presented themselves as continuing, correcting, or completing what came before. Whatever the merits of those later claims, the very fact that they arose, were accepted by large portions of the world, and claimed to address what Judaism did not — means Judaism, as a message, did not close the question. A complete message leaves nothing that requires a successor. Judaism, by the historical record, did not function that way. That alone fails criterion 4.</p>
  <p>The second failure is the one that historically requires no inference at all: the text has not been preserved intact. The Torah and wider Tanakh underwent centuries of editing, compilation, and standardization. The Dead Sea Scrolls, discovered in the twentieth century, contain versions of biblical books that diverge from the Masoretic text — the text that became the received Jewish Bible — in ways that are not trivial copying errors but substantive differences in phrasing, content, and in some cases, entire passages. The Masoretic vocalization and standardization process itself happened many centuries after the events the texts describe and many centuries after the people who first transmitted them. The tradition's own textual scholarship — not an external critique, but the work of Jewish and academic scholars working within the tradition's own sources — describes this process openly: multiple authorial strands woven together, redaction across generations, a text whose current form is the product of an editorial history.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">But the Torah is still the word of God even if it went through human transmission — why does the process of transmission invalidate it?</p>
  <p>The preservation criterion does not demand a perfect manuscript copy with zero variation. It demands an unbroken, attested chain from a single act of delivery to what is read today. What the Torah's own scholarly tradition describes is not variation in copying — it is compilation over time: different sources, different periods, different redactors. That is a categorically different thing. It means there is no single moment of delivery to trace back to. The chain does not lead to one transmission event. It leads to a process. A process is not a preserved message.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">Isn't the message still addressed to all of humanity through the Noahide laws?</p>
  <p>The Noahide framework is a rabbinic construction, not a text delivered to the nations. A universal message means the revelation itself is addressed to all of humanity — not that a subset of its obligations applies universally while the full text remains the property of one lineage. Judaism, on its own terms, was given to Israel. The rest of humanity is not its primary addressee. That is not an objection to what Judaism is on its own terms — it is a structural observation about whether it satisfies criterion 4: a complete, universal message for all human beings.</p>
