<div class="prose">
  <p>There is a precise boundary between what this chapter has done and what it has not done, and being clear about it matters more than appearing to have proven more than the argument supports.</p>
  <p>What it has done: applied five criteria, derived from reason's established attributes of the necessary being, consistently to every serious candidate. The criteria are not arbitrary — each is a logical consequence of a specific divine attribute. The application is not selective — every tradition examined was run through all five. The results are not predetermined — Islam is the candidate that remains standing after the others are set aside, but that is a finding from the application, not a premise built into it.</p>
  <p>What it has not done: the elimination of other traditions is not the same as confirming Islam. A candidate that survives a filter has not thereby been proven true — it has been identified as the one worth examining further. A job candidate who survives a background check has not been confirmed as the best hire; the next round is still required. The positive case for Islam — specific textual, historical, and evidential claims examined on their own terms — has not been made here. It is the subject of Chapter VI.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">If the positive case hasn't been made, what grounds confidence that one candidate survives, not none?</p>
  <p>None of the eliminations above depend on Islam being true. They depend on the candidate in question failing a criterion that is a logical consequence of what reason already established. The eliminations stand regardless of whether Islam's positive case succeeds. If someone showed, in Chapter VI, that Islam's miracle claim fails, the conclusion would not be that one of the earlier-eliminated traditions is vindicated — it would be that no surviving candidate was found, and the argument from a personal communicating God to any specific revelation would have failed. The elimination is not held up by Islam's positive case. Each eliminated tradition was set aside on its own grounds.</p>
  <p>The result of this chapter, stated precisely: the field of candidates compatible with everything established in Chapters I through IV has been narrowed to one. Islam in its classical, textually grounded form is the only candidate that has not been set aside by the criteria that follow from reason's account of the necessary being. Whether it deserves to be accepted — whether its specific claims hold up under scrutiny — is the question Chapter VI now takes up.</p>
