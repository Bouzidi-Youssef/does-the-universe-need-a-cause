<div class="prose">
  <p>The third criterion is preservation: an unbroken, attested chain from the text's origin to what is read today. This is not a peripheral concern. The entire force of a revelation depends on its reaching us in the form it left the one who transmitted it. A message whose wording has been edited, added to, or partially lost does not function as a reliable transmission regardless of how much of the original it still reflects. The question is not how much is preserved. It is whether what is read today is what was originally delivered — because the parts that differ or are missing carry no available marker identifying themselves as the changed or absent portions.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">Textual criticism applies to every ancient text — isn't singling Christianity out here unfair?</p>
  <p>The standard is the same for every tradition examined, but it applies with different weight depending on what the tradition itself claims. A historical chronicle that develops across centuries does not claim to be the unaltered word of God; transmission variations in it are a scholarly puzzle, not a theological problem. A revelation that claims divine authorship and demands assent on that basis carries a different burden. If the text has been altered, added to, or partially lost, the divine authorship claim becomes indeterminate: how much of what is read now is the original, and which portions are the ones that have changed? That question is not answerable from within the text itself.</p>
  <p>The New Testament manuscripts number in the thousands, and no two agree in every particular. The scholarly consensus counts several hundred thousand variant readings across the manuscript tradition, though most concern matters of spelling, word order, and minor phrasing. But some are not minor. The Gospel of John's final chapter (chapter 21) is widely noted to appear as a later addition — its own final verses (20:30–31) read structurally as the book's conclusion before the new chapter begins. The pericope adulterae (John 7:53–8:11, the woman brought before Jesus) is absent from the earliest manuscripts and appears in a variety of locations across different manuscript families — a pattern that indicates insertion rather than original composition. The longer ending of Mark (16:9–20) is similarly absent from the oldest witnesses and was added to complete an abrupt ending the tradition found unsatisfying.</p>
  <p>What the tradition's own marginal notes add is more pointed still. The example already mentioned in the preliminary discussion: in John 7:8, Jesus tells his brothers he is not going up to the feast, then goes. A marginal note in several printed editions of the Greek New Testament — the apparatus used by scholars — records "yet" as a variant reading, explaining that a copyist introduced the word to resolve what the original phrasing seemed to imply about Jesus's sincerity. The note uses the word "corruption," though the tradition frames it as a scribal attempt to protect Jesus from the appearance of a broken word rather than a deliberate falsification. The word is there; the admission that the text was changed for that reason is there too.</p>
  <p>Beyond the internal manuscript evidence, the Christian Bible makes explicit reference to books that are no longer part of it and whose contents are not extant: the Book of the Wars of the Lord (Numbers 21:14), the Book of Jasher (Joshua 10:13, 2 Samuel 1:18), the Acts of Solomon (1 Kings 11:41), the Book of Nathan the Prophet and the Prophecy of Ahijah (2 Chronicles 9:29), the Visions of Iddo the Seer, the Acts of Shemaiah the Prophet (2 Chronicles 12:15), the Annals of the Kings of Israel, the Annals of the Kings of Judah, and others referenced across Chronicles and Nehemiah. These are not apocryphal additions; they are books the text of the Bible itself cites as sources, whose contents it assumes the reader has access to. They are gone.</p>
  <p>John Chrysostom, one of the most celebrated patristic writers, noted this explicitly: that through the negligence — and in some cases the deliberate action — of the Jewish community, many prophetic books were lost. He did not treat this as a minor textual footnote but as a substantive historical fact about the tradition's record. The question is not whether this is enough to condemn Christian belief as a whole. It is whether the preservation criterion, applied consistently, is satisfied. A text that admits internal additions, variant resolutions to apparent inconsistencies, and the loss of books it explicitly cites as sources does not pass an unbroken, attested-chain test.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">Most of the New Testament is reliable — doesn't 95% fidelity still count for a great deal?</p>
  <p>The percentage framing is misleading for a specific reason. If an original message has ten parts, and nine are preserved with high fidelity while one is missing entirely, the preserved nine do not validate the missing one. The lost portion is not marked within the preserved text as the part that is missing. What was removed, added, or altered is not distinguishable from the inside of the text — it can only be inferred from manuscript comparison, and even that method requires the oldest manuscripts to survive, which in many cases they do not. A 90% preservation figure means 10% is unavailable to verify. In the specific case of books the text itself cites, the loss is total and acknowledged.</p>
