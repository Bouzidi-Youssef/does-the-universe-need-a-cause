<div class="prose">
  <p>With necessity, conditions, a testing method, and a delivery mechanism in place, the argument turns from theory to application. Running actual traditions through the criteria produces exclusions before any positive case is made for what remains.</p>
  <p>Traditions without a single, transcendent, personal God fail external consistency at the root — not on one doctrine, but against the whole framework the previous chapters built. Traditions whose central doctrine affirms plurality within the godhead, or an embodied or localised deity, conflict with the unity and transcendence already established. Traditions whose founding text is not attested as preserved intact since its origin fail the preservation condition, regardless of what else they get right.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">Isn't elimination just exclusion, not proof? A religion's endurance doesn't make it true.</p>
  <p>Agreed on both counts. Elimination narrows candidates; it does not, by itself, establish the one that remains. The positive case for whatever survives this filter — its own internal consistency, its preservation, its evidentiary claims examined on their own terms — is a separate and further argument, not a shortcut past one.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">What if a religion no one currently considers actually satisfies the criteria?</p>
  <p>The set of traditions making a serious historical claim to revelation is not unlimited, and each can in principle be run through the same six conditions. What follows here restricts itself to identifying which known traditions the framework already rules out, and which remain open to further examination.</p>
  <p class="q-lead">Judaism and Christianity also affirm one God — why do they fall on the "no" side here?</p>
  <p>By this argument's own criteria, not by dismissal: classical Trinitarian doctrine affirms three persons in one being, which the unity criterion established in Chapter III treats as internally in tension; and by this framework's preservation criterion, the textual history of both the Hebrew Bible and the New Testament is judged not to meet the standard of an unbroken, unaltered chain of transmission. Both are assessments made on stated grounds, open to being contested on those same grounds — not verdicts about the sincerity of their adherents.</p>
